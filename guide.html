🚡Your first day

New here? Try the Tutorial on the title screen: a practice resort with a checklist that walks you through a lift, a trail, a lodge and your first day open. It keeps its own save, so it won’t touch your real mountain.

  • Choose your units. Tap your resort’s name to open the menu. Units switches between imperial and metric (meters, centimeters of snow, hectares and km/h). Temperature switches independently between Fahrenheit and Celsius. Clock picks a 12-hour or 24-hour clock, and Money shows dollars or euros (only the sign changes, not the amounts). Choices update the game immediately and are remembered across resorts on this device.
  • Pick a mountain. Maple Notch is where new players start: a small, easy family hill, though with more thaws and ice. Juniper Ridge is the next step up, still friendly, with more room. Raven Peak is taller and steeper: stronger skiers, but fewer visitors, more wind and higher costs. Twin Notch has two broad summits and room for two bases along a long valley road. Lone Pine Hill is a gentle lone hill you can ski from every side, with a road from town to each side. Ridgeback has two peaks joined by a long, smooth ridge you can ski as a green, with a base under each peak.
  • Build a lift from the base. Click the cleared base area for the bottom station, then partway up for the top. Building clears trees in the way, and the price includes it.
  • Cut a trail back down from the top station to the base. Guests need a lift from the base and a trail that brings them home.
  • Build a lodge so guests can eat, warm up and use a restroom.
  • Just want to build? Start a mountain as a Sandbox (the button next to Start here when you add a mountain): unlimited money, no goals, and more land to buy from day one. Sandbox resorts stay off the leaderboards.
  • In a hurry? The welcome offers Build me a starter resort on Juniper Ridge and Maple Notch: a double chair, a green trail and a lodge built for you, ready to open. It’s all yours to change, and one undo takes it back.
  • Open for the day. A new resort starts on Dec 10 with snow already on the mountain, so once a lift, a trail and a lodge are built you can open. The holiday crowds arrive on Dec 24.
  • Later seasons start in pre-season (mid-November, bare slopes). Natural snow usually builds up by mid-December; Stay closed for a week skips ahead, stopping early for a big storm, and snowmaking gets you open sooner.
Tip Tap the What next? bulb beside your resort’s name any time. It looks at your resort and suggests the most useful next step.

🏔️Building a better mountain

Trails

  • Ratings come from steepness: green up to 25%, blue up to 40%, black up to 60%, and double black beyond. You can sign a trail differently in its panel, but guests on terrain beyond them complain and fall more.
  • Build for every ability. Guests are a mix of beginners, intermediates, advanced and experts. Strong skiers with nothing challenging get bored; beginners on steep runs get scared.
  • Width matters. Narrow trails are cheap but crowd fast; wide trails handle busy weekends, so skiers choose them more and fall less. Grooming and snowmaking cost more on wider snow: medium trails cost the standard amount, narrow ones less, wide ones more.
  • Widening and shaping a trail: tap it and choose Widen or narrow. Drag the dots on its edges to set each part’s width (the edges blend smoothly between them), or pick Narrow, Medium or Wide for the whole trail. You pay only for new ground, trees cleared and any wider bridge, plus extra snow guns if it has snowmaking. Narrowing is free, but trees don’t grow back. While the resort is open, it becomes a blueprint built that night. Snow, grooming, lights and signs stay.
  • Trails can join other trails. End a trail on another one and skiers carry on down it. Branch off the side of a trail to make a choice of routes.
  • Bridges: a trail can cross the creek; the bridge is built for you, priced by width.

Lifts

  • Surface lifts (magic carpet, T-bar) are cheap and perfect for learning areas.
  • Chairs go from double to triple to quad; express chairs move twice as fast and carry far more people per hour.
  • Gondolas are pricey, warm on cold days, the first to stop in high wind, and the only lift sightseers will ride. A gondola from the base brings in non-skiers who buy a scenic ride.
  • Patrol and injury claims: promptly rescued guests do not generate claims just for getting hurt, even in a serious glade collision or on a trail above their ability. There is a 30-minute grace period; claim risk then grows gradually with a longer wait. Missed rescues can still lead to claims. The day report explains whether claims followed missed help or a long delay. Missed rescues apply one reputation penalty, scaled by the share of injured guests who missed help, up to 5 reputation points per day. For example, missing 1 in 10 costs 0.5 points. There is no extra reputation penalty for glades or financial claims. The report shows the reduction before other reputation changes; satisfaction and other events still matter separately. Before your first patrol hut, no rescue reputation penalty applies. Open huts, enough patrollers and good hut locations help with coverage.
  • Transfers: a lift that loads near the top of another lets guests hop across without skiing down. Guests glide across level or downhill ground, or walk a short, gentle rise. Open the lower lift’s panel to see which connections are recognized. Distant or steep uphill loading stations still need another route.
  • Choosing a lift: skiers returning to the base compare open lifts again, including their lines, ride times, transfers and suitable trails. An express lift can be worth the walk. Skiers facing a long line at a mid-mountain lift, or stuck far back in any slow line, switch to a lift loading right beside it when its line is much shorter. Nearby summit lifts can share an existing trail entrance when guests can safely glide or make a short, gentle walk to it; check the lift card’s From the top list.
  • Upgrading a lift: tap it overnight and choose Upgrade lift for a triple, quad, express or gondola on the same line. You pay the difference between the two lifts plus re-rigging, and each choice shows the extra riders an hour and about how long yesterday’s line would have been. A T-bar can become a chairlift.
  • Mid-stations: open a chairlift or gondola overnight and choose Add a mid-station, then tap along its line to place it (at least 390 ft from each end, on fairly flat ground). Riders can get off there to ski the lower runs, and guests can get on there for the upper half, in seats nobody took at the bottom. Build trails from it and to it like at a lift’s ends. It costs 30% of the lift’s base price, and its crew adds a quarter to the lift’s running cost.
  • Crossing lifts: a lift may cross another. The newer one goes over on taller towers (a gondola always goes over a chairlift; T-bars and magic carpets stay underneath), and neither puts a tower in the other's path.
  • Moving a lift: open it overnight and choose Move a station, then drag the top or bottom station. It keeps its name and history and costs a quarter of the lift, plus any extra length and trees: far cheaper than tearing it down. Redraw any trails that lose their station.
  • Stations beside a trail: a station doesn’t have to sit at the very end of a trail. If a trail runs right past a bottom station, skiers can leave it to join the line, and riders getting off at a top station beside a trail ski straight onto it.
  • Trail forks: guests compare both remaining routes, including the run they are already skiing. Ability, preferred terrain, snow, crowds and where each route leads affect whether they continue or branch off. On two-base mountains, the next lift must be reachable from that base, and guests heading home favor a route back to their parking lot.
  • Editing a trail’s route: open it overnight and choose Edit route. Drag its points, or tap + between two points to add one, then check the rating and price and choose Apply route. It keeps its name, signs, lights, snow guns and shape; you pay for new clearing and for grading only the ground it didn’t already cover, and its machine snow is lost. Check the trails that join it afterward.

Learning areas

Make room on the map: use the minus button on any map card to minimize it without losing your selection or unfinished work. Undo point and Clear remain available while drawing trails and lifts, even when minimized. Expand it with the arrow. On desktop, drag the header to move the card. On phones, swipe the header up or down between minimized, compact and full views. Placement cards shrink automatically; expand for all settings and costs.

Paint a gentle slope (Trail tool › Learning area) and serve it with a carpet or T-bar. First-timers practise there before moving on to green runs, and a ski school runs bigger classes with one. Overnight, select an existing area and choose Edit learning area to add or erase ground. Added cells cost the normal preparation and tree-clearing price; trimming has no fee or refund. Keep both stations of any serving lift inside the area. Without one, first-timers end up on real trails and complain.

Guest areas at the base, mid-mountain and summit

Overnight, choose Trail › Guest area and paint a connected patch on the mountain. You can paint over trails, lift stations and building doors. Use Undo for the last stroke, Erase to trim, or Clear to start again, then confirm with Paint area. Every resort paints its first 15 acres free, then about $5,000 an acre. Painting keeps the terrain, snow and trees as they are. Any slope can be painted. Guests walk here, around buildings rather than through them, and they walk up steep ground slowly, so ground steeper than 30% shows amber. If you want them to move faster, press Level steep ground on the card to ease it (a gentle bank forms just outside the area; trails, lifts and buildings stay put). A thin outline shows the area while painting or selecting it. To fence an area off, place a mesh fence (Build › Decor) along its edge. Start painting inside an existing guest area to extend it. Build separate patches as separate areas.

Guests walk inside an area to any lift loading there, trail starting there or building door there. A lift top or trail ending inside it connects to all of them. Areas touching the low base ground count as a route home, including a trail that reaches the base through another area. A lift starting in such an area is a base lift, and rental shops, ski schools and other base buildings can go there too; guests arriving from the lot walk up through the area to reach them. The game names each area for its location; tap its label to rename it or edit its shape overnight.

Areas work with natural snow. Optional snowmaking keeps the ground white; installation and nightly costs grow with the painted size. Select an area to install it and choose Auto, Always or Off. Enlarging an equipped area charges for added equipment. Nightly running costs appear in the day report’s Snowmaking line with trail and base snowmaking.

Glades

  • A glade is forest thinned for skiing through the trees. Paint one with Trail › Glade over forest, starting at the top of a lift or beside a trail, and running out onto a trail, a lift or the base at the bottom.
  • Pick how much to thin: Tight (experts only), Classic (advanced and experts) or Open (strong intermediates too). More trees make it rate harder, and accidents likelier and more serious.
  • How tight it is comes from the trees actually standing: Plant trees inside a glade to tighten it, or clear trees to open it up.
  • Glades aren’t groomed and can’t have snowmaking, so they open on natural snow. Experts love them, most of all on powder days.
  • Tree collisions are often serious: patrol is slower to reach someone in the trees, the clinic can’t patch a serious injury, but the injury itself does not trigger a claim. Claims depend on missed help or long rescue waits. A patrol hut nearby, “Experts only” signs (on by default) and closing a glade in thin snow all help.

Terrain parks

  • Open Trail › Terrain park, pick a feature, tap a trail where it should go, and build. A green preview fits; a red one says what’s wrong. While the resort is open, features become blueprints, built overnight.
  • Tap a feature you’ve built (with nothing picked) to move it for a fifth of its price, or remove it for 25% back. Moving and removing happen overnight.
  • Small features (rollers, a flat box, a small jump) suit intermediates and up. Medium rails and the large big jump and halfpipe are for advanced skiers and experts. Features need room: a wide trail fits more side by side, and a halfpipe needs a medium or wider trail with a little pitch.
  • Park fans look for parks and ride what they can; everyone else skis around the features, and a few would rather take another trail. A bigger park draws more guests, but features take some falls, so keep a patrol hut nearby.
  • Crews reshape features each night their trail is open. The day report’s Park page shows rides, falls, fans who found nothing to ride and your most-ridden features.
Little-known Can’t afford something right now? Build it as a blueprint. While the resort is open, everything you draw becomes a plan; plans are built in order overnight when there’s enough cash. Reorder or pause them under Plans.

Buildings

  • Food: food truck, café, small, medium and large lodges, casual restaurant, fine dining, and the bar and pub for après.
  • Food trucks: the window sets the serving speed; guests eating nearby do not block new orders. Guests finish their takeaway meal first, then cold or tired skiers can visit a nearby open lodge, café or fire pit to warm up. A warm-up is a separate service, with its own price where applicable. Without a suitable nearby spot, they continue their day.
  • Venue visits: open a food venue or fire pit to see visits by service. A café’s total includes hot drinks and restroom stops as well as snacks; a food truck serves snacks. These are service visits, so one guest can count more than once. The breakdown survives a mid-day reload and appears in the last-day panel overnight. Full fire pits send warm-up guests elsewhere; guests in slow food queues may switch venues. Skiers passing food venues beside a trail compare nearby stops on that same trail before choosing a lunch line. Prices, preferences and waits still matter.
  • Base services: rental shop or rental and tuning center, ski store, gift kiosk, ski school, kids’ camp, first-aid clinic, restrooms. Rental shops, ski schools and kids’ camps come in three sizes: a big rental shop or rental hall fits more guests at once with more pairs, a ski and ride school or snowsports center has more instructors and a bigger practice area, and a kids’ center or kids’ village takes more children. Each size comes in every building style.
  • Themes: pick Classic, Modern, Scandinavian or Retro at the top of the build menu. Every building except food trucks and decor comes in all four looks. It’s only the look, so size, seats and price stay the same. Change a building’s theme for free overnight from its panel.
  • Getting here: shuttle stations and transit centers (bigger, with more bus bays), for town buses and off-site parking shuttles, are under Roads › Transit.
  • Hotels (base only): small (12 rooms, $300,000), medium (40 rooms and a restaurant, $900,000) or large (120 rooms and a restaurant, $2.4 million), in all three themes. Rooms fill night by night, most on weekends, holidays and powder days. Each booked room pays the room price (Business › Prices, $180 a night is normal) and sends two guests out of the door for the first chair. They need no car or parking space and walk back in at the end of the day. Once a day a tired hotel guest can go back to the room for a rest of 40 to 75 minutes and then ski again, instead of going home with tired legs. A base lift or a trail beside the door (ski-in, ski-out: the trail ending there or running past within about 50 m) fills more rooms, and so does a lodge, restaurant, fine dining or bar close by (cafés and food trucks don’t count). Medium and large hotels have their own restaurant (60 and 140 seats), open to every guest for sit-down lunch at the casual restaurant price and for après, which also counts as food close by; small hotels don’t. The hotel’s card, and the preview while you place one, ticks both and names the places to eat a short walk from the door. Hotel guests stay under 60% of the day’s skiers, so a small resort can’t fill a big hotel. Close a hotel on quiet days in Operations to save part of its upkeep.
  • Condos (Buildings › Lodging): a ski chalet (1 home, $220,000), a duplex (2 homes, $360,000) or a row of five townhomes ($800,000), in all three themes. Build them in the base area, or slopeside with the front door right beside a trail in the lower third of the mountain. Listed homes sell to buyers every few weeks for cash up front (the money goes straight into your cash, not into the day’s profit). A slopeside home, or one with a base lift or the end of a trail by the door, sells for 40% more. Each sold home pays dues every day, and its owners come to ski on weekends, holidays and powder days with their own passes and gear. Slopeside owners ski out of the door in the morning and, at the end of the day, ride a lift up and ski back home to it. Until a home sells it’s rented by the night for 2.5 times the hotel room price and sleeps four; turn off Sell homes on the card to keep it as a rental. Buyers are limited: about one home for every 20 skiers on an average day. Owners’ mood moves slowly with how their days on the mountain go: a few bad days barely matter, but a bad month makes them hold back dues, lowers the price of new homes and costs reputation. A condo with owners can’t be moved or removed.
  • Trees, seats and lights: trees, benches, picnic tables, ski racks, trail map boards, fire pits, lamp posts, string lights, mesh fences, holiday trees, trail lights and floodlights can go anywhere off the trails. Trees plant with one tap each. Benches, picnic tables and ski racks show a preview: tap to set the position, adjust the angle, then choose Confirm placement. Each new table or bench needs its own confirmation; while the resort is open, confirm with Plan it to save a blueprint.
  • Ski racks: skiers leave their skis on a rack before going in to eat, shop or use the restroom, and walk around in boots until they pick them up. Gift kiosks are ski-in, ski-out. Every lodge, café, restaurant, bar, ski store and restroom has a rack by its door with a space for each seat. On very busy days put an extra ski rack (Decor, 40 pairs each) within about 50 m of a crowded door: guests use it once the rack by the door is full. While you place one, a tick shows which buildings it serves. If every rack is full, skis go in the snow, which annoys guests; the day report shows where. A new resort gets a few days' grace.
  • A place to rest: tired skiers stop at benches, picnic tables and fire pits for a few minutes and head back out with a bit more energy, all day long. Put seats by lift stations and the base, or beside a trail: skiers stop as they pass. Nobody lines up for a seat: if one is taken, guests sit at a free one nearby or rest later, so a few seats together work better than one on its own.
  • Trail map boards, string lights and holiday trees: a trail map board shows your own trails and lifts and keeps itself up to date. String lights are a chain: tap where each pole goes (up to 130 ft apart), then confirm, and the bulbs hang between them. The whole chain is one piece with one card. String lights and holiday trees light up at dusk; pick warm white, holiday colours or icy blue on their card, free, any time. They stay that way until you change them. A few by a bar, lodge or restaurant draw slightly more guests to après.
  • Mesh fences: tap where each post goes (up to 130 ft apart, up to 30 posts), then confirm, and plastic mesh is strung between them along the ground. It costs $120 a post plus a little for each length of mesh, and $1 a post a day in upkeep. It starts orange; pick blue, red, green or black on its card, free, any time, and your next fence starts in that colour. A fence is just for looks: guests and skiers aren't stopped by it, and it can cross a trail, though its posts can't stand on one. It replaces the old boundary fence switch on guest areas.
  • Bulldoze (Landscape, or press B) removes one thing per tap: a single tree (planted or wild), a bench, a picnic table, a trail light, a floodlight, a lamp post, string lights, a holiday tree, a trail map board, a fire pit, a mesh fence (the whole fence) or a snow gun model. Hiding a snow gun is free and keeps its snowmaking effect and costs. Undo restores it. Handy for opening a view or tidying a spot without the big brush.
  • Level ground (Landscape): press where the ground should be level, then drag. Your changes show straight away, red-brown where ground is dug out and gold where it’s built up, with the price so far. Use Undo stroke to step back, Before and After to compare, Put it all back to undo the lot exactly, or Keep it when you’re happy. Kept leveling can still be undone overnight with Undo. In a match, each stroke is made straight away.
  • Plant trees (Landscape) paints trees in: where trees were cleared it regrows them ($150 a tree), and on open ground that never had any (meadows, the base, high ground) it plants new ones ($400 a tree, like a single Tree from Buildings). Paint over a gap between trails to fill it back in, or inside a glade to tighten it; trails, lift lines, learning areas, glade lines, buildings, the road and the lot stay clear. The brush works right up to your property line.
  • Buying land: after your first week open, Business › Land lists the 24 parcels round your property. Buy one, or lease it for a nightly rent; permits take three days. Road frontage has room for shuttle stations and a village, valley ground suits a second base, and mountain parcels add terrain. Low ground on the far side of a hill from your parking lots isn’t part of the base: guests reach it by skiing, not by walking over the top. A lease can be handed back once nothing of yours stands on it.
  • Precise rotation: use the rotation slider or enter an angle from 0–359° while placing a building, bench or picnic table. Use Left 90° to turn counterclockwise or Right 90° to turn clockwise. R also turns it quickly. To turn one already built, open its card overnight and choose Rotate it, then confirm. Turning has no fee, but any extra clearing and leveling is shown before you confirm. Bus stations snap toward the road; trail lights face the trail.
  • Moving a building: open its panel overnight and choose Move it. It keeps its name, size and history; it costs a fifth of what it took to build, plus clearing and leveling.

⛷️Keeping guests happy

  • Every guest is simulated. Tap anyone on the mountain to read their thoughts: they’ll tell you about long lines, scary runs, cold, hunger and great days. A name tag marks them, and Follow with the camera on their card keeps the view on them until you drag the map or close the card. Anyone you follow (up to three a day) gets their own page in the day report: their whole day from then on, line by line, with each lift and how long they waited, each run, lunch, their thoughts and when they went home.
  • The day report lists what went well and what didn’t, ranked by importance. The top notes are usually your best next moves.
  • Lift lines are the biggest complaint. Add a lift, upgrade to an express, or spread guests out with more trails.
  • Rentals: many beginners need rental gear when they arrive. Without a rental shop they rent in town and arrive annoyed.
  • What are lodges for? Lodges serve quick-service lunch, hot drinks and warming breaks, restrooms, and après drinks. They do feed hungry guests; a restaurant is not required. Small, medium and large lodges have 120, 360 and 800 seats, with faster food counters at each size. Restaurants offer longer sit-down meals at a separate price.
  • An empty lodge? Put it near a busy lift station or trail where hungry guests pass (a bigger lodge has a bigger footprint, so check its door still sits by the lift or trail), and check that it is open in the day’s Operations plan. Lunch-only buildings open from 11 AM to 2 PM. Check Prices › Lodge meal for lodge meals; restaurant meals use the separate dining price. Tap the lodge to see meals, hot drinks and restroom visits separately. Guests can try another type of meal after rejecting a price, retry a rejected meal category if you lower its price. Waiting longer does not change their budget decision. Guests already eating elsewhere will finish that visit, and new buildings planned during the day open after they are built overnight.
  • Lunch rush: around midday everyone wants to eat at once. The report tells you when the rush peaked and how many guests ate without a long delay, ate after a long delay, or left without eating. A long delay means waiting more than 75 minutes past their preferred lunch time before getting food. Guests who left without eating have a reason recorded: price, long lines, or no food found on their route. Guests leaving before their lunch time, night skiers and sightseeing visitors are excluded from these meal totals. Hungry skiers look for food at lift stations and beside trails, and choose connected trails and operating lifts toward a meal. They avoid food routes above their ability and do not walk straight across to another base. Meal searches continue until their ski session ends, including after 3 PM. Guests heading home after their lunch time without having eaten stop at a base eatery on the way to the car, while the lifts are running.
  • Restrooms matter more than you think, especially at the top of busy lifts.
  • Ski patrol reaches injured guests; the closer the hut, the faster. There is one patrol hut in the build menu, starting with two patrollers. Select a hut overnight to choose 1–6 patrollers, with no expansion fee. Daily cost is $100 per hut plus $425 per selected patroller ($525 for 1, $950 for 2, and $2,650 for 6). Patroller wages are included once under Staff in the day report, with a separate breakdown; the hut’s $100 is building upkeep. Closed huts pay only the $100 upkeep. Existing teams keep their size. In Operations, turn on Override all patrol huts and choose 1–6 patrollers per hut for that plan. Turn it off to restore each hut’s manual choice. Changes during an open day apply from tomorrow. Every selected patroller is on duty when the hut is open. Lean, Normal and Extra affect other employees, not patrol numbers or pay. Returning patrollers take operating lifts uphill and occupy a seat; when no lift route reaches home, they remain available at the aid station. A clinic patches many injured guests up so they ski again.
  • Families drop the kids at a kids’ camp and stay longer.
  • Lessons grow your skiers: beginners who take lessons come back better. A busy ski school slowly turns a few of your beginners into intermediates who ski your blue runs. It fades by half each new season unless you keep teaching.

Parking and getting here

  • When your lots fill, guests are turned away. Draw more parking in the Roads tool, or set up off-site parking.
  • Roads, parking and transit have their own tool, Roads (key 6), with three tabs. It’s new and a big change, so if something doesn’t work as you expect, please report it under Menu › Feedback & feature ideas.
  • Parking: drag on the map to draw a lot of any size. It lines up with the nearest road, and shows its spaces and price as you go: $781 a space, plus levelling and any trees. Drag its handles to move it, turn it to any angle or change its size; tap an edge to add or take away an entrance. Draw a lot against another to add on to it: they join, and cars drive through. Tap a lot to change it, or remove it for a quarter back. Every lot can go, the main lot too; then guests come only by bus, off-site shuttle or from your hotels.
  • Roads: drag or tap from the access road, or one of your roads, to start a new one, and end it on a lot's edge: an entrance opens there and cars drive in. Choose a two-lane road ($2,000 every 33 ft) or a narrower driveway ($1,200), plus any trees in the way; a bridge over the creek costs extra. Roads can climb 16% at most, so wind them across a slope: as you draw, the panel shows the steepest part and any too-steep stretch turns red. They can't go through buildings, lots, lift stations, guest areas or ponds. To reroute a road, pick it from the list in the Roads tab (or tap one of yours and choose Change it instead), then drag its points; the access road only moves inside your land. Roads are plowed every day, a little for each length.
  • Transit: shuttle stations and transit centers go beside the access road, and off-site parking takes the overflow when your lots are full.
  • Your lots park guests for the nearest base; they never make a new base. In Operations, close whole lots on quiet days to save their running cost. King of the Mountain keeps its parking sections for now.
  • Off-site parking with a shuttle station takes the overflow; a town bus brings guests with no car at all.
  • Guests use the rental shop, school or clinic nearest to where they parked or got off the bus.

More than one base

  • On Twin Notch you start at the main lot at the end of the valley road, under the east summit. Build the West lot (Parking) to open a second base under the west summit.
  • On Lone Pine Hill you start at the main lot on the sunny south side. Build the North lot (Parking) to open a second base round the back, under the steeper north face. Its road forks off in town, so base shuttles go the long way round by the fork.
  • On Ridgeback you start at the main lot under the beginner peak. Build the East lot to open a base under the expert peak; its road splits off at the crossroads in the valley.
  • Guests spread across all your lots from the start, leaning toward the lifts and runs that suit them and the lifts they liked best on earlier days. Emptier lots draw more cars, so the lots fill up together. A lot with no lifts they’d ski stays a last resort.
  • Link the bases so guests can ski both: a village gondola (Lifts, with one station at each base) or a shuttle station at each base, where base shuttle buses run along the road. More buses run as the lines at the stops grow (up to six), and when the stops are busy fewer guests bother to cross. Without a link, guests only ski the lifts where they parked, and anyone who ends up at the wrong base waits a long time for a ride back to their car.
  • At closing, guests head back to the base where they parked. The village gondola keeps running until après is over, so nobody is left at the wrong base. The day report shows guests at each base and how often they crossed.
  • Bases of your own, on any mountain: a parking lot you build (Roads › Parking) with a road to it starts a new base when it’s over a hill from your other bases, or well away from them. A lot beside a base is part of that base. Link a base of your own with a village gondola or shuttle stations like any other. If it’s on the same low ground as another base, guests can also just walk over. Build as many as you like; guests change bases more than once if that’s the way to where they’re going.

📋Running the resort

Under Business you’ll find Operations, Prices, Events, Evenings and Reputation.

  • Track reputation: open Business › Reputation (or More › Reputation on phones) for your current score, last day’s gains and losses, and a 14-operating-day trend. Scores range from 50 to 150; 100 means normal demand. The panel records actual changes after those limits, including overnight emergency borrowing. Older days keep their saved scores but may have no breakdown; new reasons are recorded from this update onward.
  • Plan from the map: select a building or lift to edit its Weekday, Weekend and Quiet season settings directly on its card. Buildings offer Open and Closed, plus Lunch for eateries; lifts offer Full, Slow and Off. These are the same settings shown in Operations. Changes during an open day apply from tomorrow. A manually stopped lift still needs to be started.
  • Which trails are popular? The day report’s Trails page shows the five most-skied trails and glades, how much of the day’s skiing each took, and open trails hardly anyone skied. In the Trails list, Sort: Popular ranks them by runs (today’s while open, the last day’s overnight). Glades are in the list too, under their own tab.
  • Which buildings pay their way? In the Buildings list, Sort: Profit ranks them by the last day’s profit, and Sort: By type groups them (all the cafés together, and so on) with each group’s total, best first; tap a type’s heading to fold its list away. The list opens where you left it. Profit map colours the ground round each building: red lost money, yellow about broke even, green earned the most, and its label shows the figure. Tap it again to compare each building only with others of its type (the best café is green even if lodges earn more), and a third time to turn it off. Restrooms, patrol huts, clinics and bus stations earn nothing of their own, so they aren’t coloured. While it’s on, the Buildings list has a chip for each type: tap one (condos, say) to leave that type off the map.
  • Where skiers were all day: press the Traffic button (or T) once for crowding right now and again for the whole day, section by section along each trail. Overnight it shows the last day, so you can plan new trails around the red spots. A third press turns it off.
  • Lift colors: select a lift and choose one of eight colors on its card. Changes are free and immediate, update the lift and map label, and stay with your save. Guests already riding or queuing are unaffected.
  • Three plans: weekdays, weekends and holidays, and the quiet ends of the season. Each sets which lifts run (full, slow or off), which buildings open (or lunch only), parking sections, hours, staffing and patrol. Closing parking sections here only shuts them for those days: they stay built and reopen whenever you want. Removing a section for good is under Parking.
  • Cut back on quiet days, not busy ones. Guests expect more on weekends, at higher prices and as your reputation grows. The plan shows how much guests will notice.
  • Patrol headcount sets how many rescuers are available. Guests who cannot get help may file an injury claim; there is no separate Full/Skeleton setting.
  • Prices: charge more on weekends and holidays. Guests compare every price with what’s normal; charge too much and some will pass.
  • Bigger resorts can charge more. A small hill’s normal lift ticket is about $65. More trails, more lift capacity and a good reputation raise it, up to about $120 for the biggest resorts. Prices shows today’s figure for your resort, and season pass buyers follow it too.
  • Season passes sell once, on your first open day of the season. Holders ski free about eight days each, mostly busy ones.
Tip Turn on Auto-advance in the day report to run up to a week of days in a row. It stops for anything important.

❄️Snow and weather

  • Each trail needs enough base to open (steeper trails need more). The Snow panel shows every trail’s base and the forecast.
  • Snowmaking runs on cold nights and opens trails early in the season. Use Auto with a target depth so the guns don’t run all winter.
  • Spring skiing: after March 31 you can keep opening as long as a lift from the base and trails with enough snow still bring skiers back down to the base, as late as May 31. Snowmaking stops after April 30, so May runs on the snow that’s left. Fewer people come in May. If a thin spring day isn’t worth opening for, end the season early in Manage › Snow.
  • Water for snowmaking: village water runs up to 120 snow guns a night. With more guns than that wanting to run, each makes its share of snow. Dig a snowmaking pond (Landscape › Pond, or Manage › Snow) for more: paint fairly flat ground away from trails, the road, buildings and lift stations. Lifts can pass over a pond: their towers stand in the water. Each acre adds about 60 guns of water that costs about 45% less to pump. A pond freezes in the cold and has its pumphouse on the bank. Tap it to see your water, or to fill it back in. King of the Mountain has no water limit for now.
  • Base snowmaking keeps the base area white. A bare, muddy base costs you satisfaction and stops lessons.
  • Grooming is nightly by default. Leave a trail ungroomed for powder after storms (and moguls later): strong skiers love it.
  • Powder days (6 inches or more) bring a crowd. Be open.
  • Wind holds stop exposed lifts in the morning; winds often ease by noon.
  • Bad snow? Icy trails double the chance of a fall, and thin cover adds about 60%. In the Trails list, Close icy and thin closes all of them (glades too) in one go, and Reopen good ones brings back just those once their snow recovers. Trails you closed by hand stay closed. Open and Close buttons under the list act on every trail the current tab shows.

🌙Après-ski and night skiing

Set both under Business › Evenings, separately for each plan.

  • Après: after the lifts close, the bar, lodge, fine dining and base or village restaurants, cafés and food trucks stay open (cafés and trucks until 7 PM). Up the mountain, a lodge, bar or fine dining beside a village gondola station does après all evening. With night skiing on, a lodge, restaurant, bar or fine dining beside a lit trail does too, until 15 minutes after the lights go out: night skiers stop in on their last run down. Anywhere else up the mountain, nobody can get there once the lifts close. Guests who had a good day stop in, more on weekends and sunny afternoons, and most of all with a bar.
  • Last call: 7 PM, 9 PM, 11 PM or 1 AM. The lodge stops serving at 8 and restaurants at 10; the bar and fire pits stay until last call. Late nights pay staff time and a half after 10, plus security.
  • Night owls and locals: with a late last call, night owls go round again and locals drive up just for the bar. Late on weekends usually pays; late on weeknights usually doesn’t.
  • Light security is cheaper, but a rowdy night past 11 can cost you reputation.
  • Fire pits draw a free après crowd, best within a short walk of a bar or lodge.
  • Night skiing: light a trail from its panel (overnight), then pick night hours. Only lit trails run after dark, and only lifts night skiers can reach from the base by lit trails and other night lifts, with a lit trail off the top that leads home. Locals buy night tickets after work; some day guests stay on. Cafés, food trucks, lodges and casual restaurants stay open for them, wherever they are, along with restrooms and rentals; hungry night skiers can sit down for dinner at a casual restaurant. All ski lifts stop boarding at night closing time; riders already aboard finish their trip. Lights stay on while the last night guests clear the mountain, and that extra time adds to operating costs.
  • Trail lights (Buildings › Lights and fire pits) fill any dark stretch on a lit trail.
  • Floodlights (Buildings › Decor) light a wide patch of snow. Poles along a trail light its edges, so a very wide trail can be dark in the middle: place a floodlight beside it and it turns to face the trail. Rotate it while placing it, or later from its card, to aim it yourself. Each one uses about as much power as two or three trail lights.
  • An automatic pole in an odd spot, like where two trails merge? Bulldoze it for free. It stays gone and the trail stays lit, so you can put your own exactly where you want.
Little-known Once the lifts are closed and night skiing is over, the top button becomes Skip to last call. And the day report has its own Après and Night pages, with après revenue hour by hour.

☀️Summer

When the season ends the snow melts, and the Snow button becomes Summer. You can open for summer guests day by day, or skip straight to next winter: the summer upkeep is paid either way, a share each summer day.

  • Full or condensed: a full summer is every day from late June to the fall colours in October. Condensed plays 18 busy days, Friday to Sunday at six points in the summer, and each counts 2.5 times so both come out about the same. Switch any morning in the Summer panel.
  • Open the weekends: weekdays are quiet. Stay closed until Friday skips ahead, paying each closed day’s upkeep. The Fourth of July, Labor Day and the fall colours are the busiest days.
  • Scenic rides: summer guests ride a chairlift or gondola from the base for the view, wander or eat at the top, and ride back down. A café or restaurant by the top station brings more of them. In early October the trees turn gold.
  • The Summer plan (Business › Operations) picks which lifts and buildings run. It starts with one lift. Running the whole mountain for a few hundred sightseers loses money.
  • Weddings and events: build an event pavilion (Buildings › Summer). A lawn is cheapest but rain spoils it; the pavilion and hall have roofs. Bookings arrive in the Summer panel: accept the ones you can host. A restaurant, lodge or hotel restaurant nearby does the catering, or an outside caterer takes a cut. A pavilion by a lift top pays more, but that lift must run. You can also book a summer concert, and a fall festival for the colours weekend.
  • Zip line: under Lifts, tap a top tower beside the top of a chairlift or gondola from the base, then a bottom tower in a base. Guests at the top pay to fly down.
  • Bike park: under Trails › Bike trail, tap from beside a lift top down to a base. Go across the slope: a trail straight down is too steep, and gentler trails are rated green or blue. Then select the lift and Fit bike carriers (gondolas take bikes as they are), and make sure it runs in the Summer plan. Riders buy a day ticket and lap the trails.
  • Trail crew: bike trails wear with every lap and every wet day, and rough trails slow riders down and spoil their fun. Each trail’s crew fixes most of the wear overnight; turn it off in the trail’s panel to save its cost.
  • Summer in review: when summer ends, a report shows what earned the most. A summer that made money adds to the season before it on the Best season leaderboard.
Tip Bike riders stay all day and fill your parking. If the day report says guests were turned away, open more parking before you add attractions. Prices for scenic rides, the zip line and the bike park are under Business › Prices.

💰Money

  • Every day has a report: revenue and expenses line by line, and the Money panel keeps your history and season totals.
  • Season charts: Money charts guests, profit, satisfaction and cash for every open day of the season. Hover or tap a chart to read a day.
  • Why today: the top of each day report compares guests with your last week of open days, says what made the difference, and names the biggest leak to fix.
  • Problems on the map: while open, amber and red tags mark long lift lines, a full lot and slow building lines. Tap one to open it; switch them off under Map labels.
  • The bank lends against your reputation and your best season. Interest is charged every night, including closed nights and summer.
  • Overhead runs every day; before your first open day each season it’s at a lower, off-season rate.
  • In trouble? If the bank calls in the loan, a certain investor in a fur coat may offer to pay it off for a share of everything you earn. You can buy him out later, at a price.
  • Selling: removing a trail, lift or building returns a quarter of what it cost.

👑King of the Mountain (beta)

King of the Mountain is multiplayer. Two to four real players each run a resort on one shared mountain, and the resort with the most profit at the end wins. A match keeps running day and night, so you play it a little at a time: check in on your phone or your computer, signed in to the same account, whenever you like. Tap King of the Mountain on the title screen to start.

The mountains

Each resort gets its own slice of the mountain, from its own base and parking lot up to a flat summit. The slices have their own characters, but they’re made about as good as each other: the same acres, about the same vertical and the same trees to clear. Your game always shows your slice in front, and your rivals’ lifts and trails show beside it.

  • Crown Peak: 2 or 3 resorts, each with a slice of one round mountain, up to a shared summit.
  • Ridgeline: 2 to 4 resorts side by side, each in its own valley below one long ridge. Each valley starts with a long winding green, a blue and a black from the top, and a first row of parking that needs no land. One has a steep lower half, one a steep headwall, one chutes and one a bench halfway up.
  • Willow Valley (new): 2 to 4 resorts, two on each side of a valley with the road and a river down the middle, so you look across at your rivals. Each slice has a creek down each side and room for 4 + 4 parking sections.

Land for sale is only in front of your own slice: the valley below it, two parcels either side of your base road on Ridgeline, and on Willow Valley three more plots on the mountainside past your end of the valley.

Joining a match

  • Sign in and pick a player name. Every name is one of a kind. Rivals see it in the standings and the chat, and tapping a name opens that player’s profile.
  • Real players only: there are no computer players. An empty slice stays empty until someone joins.
  • Official tournaments are run by Mountain Mogul, and the top three get a badge on their profile.
  • Player matches: any signed-in player can press Make a match and pick its name, the mountain (or tap a mountain’s picture), how many resorts, how often a new day starts, how many seasons, when it starts, whether late joining is on, and an optional password so only friends can join. You can have one match of your own at a time, and cancel it before it starts. Player matches don’t give badges.
  • Joining late: unless it’s turned off, you can join a running match until a quarter of the way through. You get a catch-up investment of $15,000 for each day you missed (up to $1 million), as cash to build with.
  • You can be in up to three matches at once.

How a match day works

  • A new day every 5, 10, 15 or 30 minutes, for one, two or three seasons. One season takes about 14 hours with 5-minute days, 28 hours with 10, 42 hours with 15 and 3½ days with 30. Between days, the top bar counts down to the next one.
  • Watch it live. Your day plays on the match screen, faster in shorter turns so it always fits. In a 30-minute turn the lifts run until 4 PM, about 18 minutes in, then night skiing and après play out by about 28 minutes (in a 10-minute turn, about 6 and 9 minutes). Everyone shares one clock, so the evening runs until the last resort closes. If nobody runs an evening, the day ends at 5:15 PM.
  • Your money moves as you watch: tickets in the morning, food at lunch, then wages and upkeep at closing, when it lands on the server’s official figure.
  • Skiers choose each morning. The town’s skiers pick a resort by price, terrain, snow and reviews. Price is weighed against what a resort of that size usually charges (Prices shows yours), so a big resort can charge more and still win skiers. A cheap ticket draws a crowd, but a full resort turns skiers away to its rivals. Long runs count for more: a trail about 600 m long counts as one, a long top-to-bottom run up to two, a short link half. Season passes are bought on opening day and shared by how much each resort draws.
  • Keep an eye on your rivals: their skiers ride their lifts and ski their trails, and their parking fills with cars, about one for every two or three skiers. A busy lot means a busy resort.

Building and running your resort

  • Almost everything from home works, even while the day plays: lifts, trails, buildings, parking, terrain parks, learning and guest areas, land, levelling, planting trees, prices, operating plans, grooming, snowmaking, ski patrol, loans and events. So do lift upgrades, mid-stations, off-site parking, building themes, moving lifts and buildings, and editing or widening trails.
  • New things open the next morning. What you build during the day shows as a blueprint until it’s built that night. Things you remove keep running until tonight. A lift upgrade is paid for straight away and done overnight. Price changes are sent within a few seconds; other settings apply from the next day.
  • Blueprints work too: the match server builds them overnight, in your order, when there’s cash.
  • Some tools pause your live day while you use them: removing a parking section, moving a lift or a building, mid-stations, off-site parking, editing a trail and the terrain park tool.
  • Quiet days: set an operations plan’s Hours to Stay closed to save money. You don’t open on those days, your skiers go to your rivals, and you can watch your rivals’ day instead.
  • Spring: the same spring rule as at home. You can end your season early from Manage › Snow, and it ends the next morning. When a spring day loses money, a message offers to open on weekends and holidays only, to end your season, or to keep going. (Matches started before version 3.1 keep the old rule: an 18-inch average trail base.)
  • Summer in a longer match: after a season’s last day, every resort shuts for the summer together. You get one turn to build, the summer night pays your summer upkeep, and the next season starts on November 15 with thin snow, so watch the forecast and make snow. Each later season runs to late May.

Winning

The resort with the most season profit wins. Profit is what your resort earns by running each day: lift tickets, passes, food, shops, lessons, lodging and the rest, minus wages, upkeep and other running costs. Building never counts against you, so spending on a new lift today doesn’t cost you the match. In a match of two or three seasons, every season’s profit adds up, summer upkeep included, and the standings show each season’s share.

Condos and profit Selling condo homes gives you cash to build with, but home sales don’t count as profit, so building and selling condos won’t win you a match. What does count is what condos earn as part of the resort: the dues owners pay every day and the nightly rent from homes that haven’t sold yet. Their owners also ski with you, filling your lifts and spending in your lodges.
  • Badges and wins: in official tournaments the top three get a badge. Every match you finish counts on your profile as played, and a win counts when at least two real players took part.
  • When a match ends, the results come up on the title screen the next time you open the game: where you came, the final standings, your resort’s numbers and any badge. Tap Look at the mountain to walk around it. Finished matches stay under Past matches in the King of the Mountain window.

Leaving and claiming a resort

  • Leaving: the menu’s Leave this match takes the place of Start this mountain over. Your resort stays on the mountain, shut, with no skiers and no costs. It counts on your profile as Abandoned, the match doesn’t count as played, and you can’t come back to it or claim another resort in that match.
  • Claiming: a resort someone left shows as free to claim, with its cash, debt, profit, lifts, trails and guests so far. Any signed-in player who isn’t in that match can claim it until three-quarters of the way through. You take it over that night, and it reopens the next morning.
  • A fresh start: the first claim of a resort gets help from the bank: its debt is written off, its cash goes up to at least $500,000, a season loss goes back to $0 (a profit is kept) and its reputation goes back to at least a new resort’s. Only one fresh start per resort per match.
  • A badge or a win only counts if you ran the resort for at least half the match. Each claim counts on your profile as a resort rescued.

The match menu, watching and chat

  • The crown button on the left opens the match menu: standings, chat, past day reports, pausing the live day and going back to the title. It turns orange with a count when there’s new chat.
  • Watching: press Watch on a match you’re not in. You can look around every resort; the numbers, reports and live day are for the resort you follow. Press Next resort in the crown menu, or Follow in the standings, to switch. On Ridgeline and Willow Valley the game reloads onto that resort’s own view.
  • Chat is for everyone in the match and signed-in watchers, with match news in the same feed. Keep it friendly: messages go through a word filter, and you can mute or report anyone.
  • It’s a beta: please report anything odd from the title screen’s feedback card, and we’ll fix it fast.

🏆Leaderboards and visiting

  • Sign in (title screen › Community) to save your mountains online, play on any device and appear on the leaderboards.
  • Leaderboards rank busiest day, best day, best season, all-time guests and biggest resort, across all mountains or filtered to one.
  • Visit anyone’s resort read-only and press Watch a day to see it run. Nothing you do there changes it. The eye button at the top left (or the mountain name beside it) shows how many likes the resort has, with Like and Back to title.
  • Likes pay: each new like on your resort pays a $100,000 bonus, up to 10 a season. A staff pick pays $1,000,000.
  • Five mountains: you can keep up to five resorts going at once. Their title-screen tiles show scenic photos of your resorts. Open an older resort once to refresh its picture; photos follow your online saves to other devices.

✏️Make your own mountain

  • Open it from Custom mountains on the title screen (Make your own), or from Make your own when you start a new mountain. Name it, then start from a blank slate (gently tilted land) or My mountains › Edit this mountain to change one of the game’s.
  • Shape the land: pick Raise, Lower, Smooth, Flatten or Roughen and drag on the ground; hold still to keep going. Wide reach brings the land around up too: rough out the mountain with it, then turn it off for ridges, gullies and more peaks. On a phone, tap the tool again to put it down and look around.
  • Add to it: drag a Stream from its spring down, drag the Access road from the edge of the map to where the base should be (the parking lot goes at its end), paint Ponds on fairly flat ground, and paint the forest with Plant trees and Clear trees (trees still keep off the road, streams and ponds). A resort on your mountain starts with its ponds dug, pumphouse and all, for free; running snow guns on them costs as usual.
  • Weather and money: set snowfall, wind, thaws, how far the town is (nearer means bigger crowds and lower costs) and the starting money. Until you do, the mountain has the weather of the one it started from.
  • Ready to play when the checklist is green: a road to a base on the lowest ground of the land (base ground is where lifts start and guests walk), flat room there for parking and a lodge, and at least 500 feet of vertical. Show flies you to a problem. Press Play this mountain to start a resort on it, or tick Sandbox first.
  • Share it once you’re signed in with a player name: you get a link anyone can open, guests too. Choose whether others may copy it to edit. A shared mountain never changes, so resorts on it are safe: keep shaping and share again for a new link. You can stop sharing any time.
  • Custom mountains lists every shared mountain by newest, likes, rating or resorts started, with a search. Open one to Look around it in 3D (Steepness shows the greens, blues and blacks), like it, give it 1 to 5 stars, and play it or copy it.
  • Start over puts a mountain back as it began. Your editor keeps 8 mountains on this device.

Invite friends

Choose Invite friends in your resort’s dropdown menu to take a mountain photo and share your invitation link. A new player must follow your link before creating a resort, confirm their account, and complete 7 operating days across at least 3 real days. Completed days must save online; real days follow the server’s UTC calendar.

Each qualifying friend earns you $100,000. Claim it into the regular resort you are playing after the day ends. Your account can receive three rewards total ($300,000), across all resorts. Three qualifying friends also earn a special decoration milestone; the decoration will arrive in a future update.

The invitation card appears once per in-game season, from December 20 through the end of February. You can open it from the menu at any time. Returning players, self-invitations and unconfirmed guest accounts do not qualify.

✨Hidden gems

  • Moving the view: drag to move and scroll or pinch to zoom. To turn and tilt, right-drag on a computer (or hold Alt and drag on a trackpad), or on a phone twist two fingers to turn and slide two fingers up or down to tilt. On a phone, double-tap to zoom in, tap with two fingers to zoom out, and flick to glide across the map. Q E still turn an eighth at a time, and re-centring puts the tilt back.
  • Map labels by type: the tag button by the map controls hides trail, lift or building labels separately.
  • Trail colour edges (menu) show each trail’s rating as a coloured edge; turn them off for a cleaner look.
  • Music (menu): calm songs while you build. It’s off until you turn it on. Pause, skip to the next song and set the volume right in the menu. Your choice is remembered on this device, and the music goes quiet while the game is in another tab. The songs were made for Mountain Mogul with Google Flow Music.
  • Graphics (menu): if your device struggles, a lighter setting keeps it smooth.
  • Help bulb off: know your way around? Hide it in the menu. That also hides the circles that suggest where a new resort’s first lift goes.
  • Friday defaults: turn on Use weekend plan on Fridays in Operations to use that plan every Friday, including quiet season. It is saved with your resort and applies from tomorrow if the resort is open. One-day choices still take priority, and event days always use the weekend plan. Ticket prices and crowd demand are unchanged.
  • Plan a single day differently: in Operations, pick another plan for just one day (say, the weekend plan for a holiday Friday).
  • Fire pit without a stone base: turn off Stone base in its panel for just the ring and log benches on the snow.
  • Raise the base: a building on a slope can be levelled higher from its panel, overnight.
  • Orange dots mark where new features live after an update, until you tap them. The version number on the title screen lists every change.
  • Your phone stays awake while the game runs, so a long day at 1× won’t lock the screen.
